How to read this table

Accuracy higher is better

Share of games where the side given more than 50% won. "Always home" picks the home team every time, so its {{ '%.1f' % (home.accuracy * 100) }}% is simply how often home teams won in {{ r.test_info.season }}.

It ignores confidence: a 51% pick and a 95% pick count the same.

Log loss lower is better

Average over the {{ model.n }} games of −ln(probability given to what happened).

Home team at 84%: a home win costs 0.174, a home loss costs 1.833. A 50% guess always costs 0.693; a 99% pick that misses costs 4.605. Confident misses are punished hardest.

"Always home" gives every game 55.8%, the training (2006–2023) home-win rate.

Brier lower is better

Average of (p − result)², where the result is 1 for a home win and 0 for a loss.

Home team at 84%: a home win costs 0.026, a home loss costs 0.706. A 50% guess always costs 0.250. Gentler on confident misses than log loss.

Scale: a coin flip scores 0.693 log loss and Vegas {{ '%.3f' % vegas.log_loss }}, so all the skill fits in a gap of under 0.09. On this scale the model's {{ '%.3f' % (model.log_loss - vegas.log_loss) }} behind Vegas is a meaningful difference, not a rounding detail.

// Calibration

When it says 70%, does it happen 70% of the time?

How to read these charts

Games are grouped by their predicted home-win probability into 10 bins (0.1–0.2, 0.2–0.3, …). Each point is one bin: across is the average prediction, up is how often the home team actually won.

The dotted diagonal is perfect calibration: 70% predictions win 70% of the time.

Above the line: the home team won more often than predicted (the forecast was too low). Below: it won less often (too high).

In 2025 both lines follow the diagonal from about 0.5 up. Under 0.3 the model's points sit above it: the home teams it rated least likely to win (17–25%) actually won about a third of the time. Between 0.3 and 0.5 its points sit a little below. The end bins hold few games, so single points there are noisy.

How to read this chart

Each bar is a feature's coefficient after scaling every feature to standard deviations, so the bars can be compared. It is the change in the home team's log-odds when that home-minus-away difference grows by one standard deviation, with the other features held fixed.

Right of zero helps the home team, left hurts it. Longer bar = more influence.

Example: in an average matchup the model gives the home team 56.5%. Give the home team a one-SD edge in Elo (about 152 points) and the log-odds rise by 0.625 (odds ×1.87), so the probability goes to 70.8%. A one-SD edge in recent point differential (about 10.8 points per game) moves it only to 60.8%.

The small bars (rest, turnovers, division, neutral site) each move the probability by only 1–2 percentage points per standard deviation.

// Limitations

What it can't see.

  • No injuries, quarterback changes or weather.
  • Small playoff sample: about 13 games a season.
  • Season simulator uses simplified tiebreakers and does not simulate turnovers.
  • Only ~5 neutral-site games a season, so the neutral-site effect is poorly estimated. Neutral games are averaged over both home/away labelings so neither team is favored by the label.
